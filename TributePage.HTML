Marie Curie

Scientist. Two-time Nobel Prize winner. The only woman in the room.

Marie Curie, photographed in the 1920s. Source: Wikimedia Commons (public domain)

Marie Sklodowska Curie moved from Warsaw to Paris in 1891 to study at the Sorbonne. She finished first in her class in physics, and first again in math two years after that. Working with her husband Pierre in a small lab, she discovered two new elements: polonium, named after her home country Poland, and radium.

Her work wasn't easy or quick. She had to process tons of ore by hand just to get a small amount of pure radium, and she kept at it for years even though it slowly damaged her health.

Born: November 7, 1867, Warsaw, Poland

Nobel Prize (Physics): 1903, shared with Pierre Curie and Henri Becquerel

Nobel Prize (Chemistry): 1911, won alone

Discovered: Polonium and radium

Died: July 4, 1934

Timeline

1891

Moves to Paris

Enrolls at the Sorbonne after studying in secret back home, since women weren't allowed into Poland's regular universities at the time.

1898

Discovers polonium and radium

Comes up with the word "radioactivity" to describe what she's seeing in her research.

1903

Wins her first Nobel Prize

Becomes the first woman to ever win a Nobel Prize, in Physics, shared with Pierre and Henri Becquerel.

1911

Wins her second Nobel Prize

This time in Chemistry, and this one is hers alone. No one else has ever won Nobel Prizes in two different sciences.

1914-1918

Builds mobile X-ray units

During WWI she personally set up X-ray vehicles (called "petites Curies") and trained women to run them near the front lines.

What Made Her Great

  • She didn't accept answers she couldn't prove. Instead of just assuming radioactivity was a chemical reaction, she spent years measuring it exactly.
  • She didn't patent her discovery. She and Pierre could have made a fortune from the radium extraction process but chose to let other scientists use it freely instead.
  • She kept going after Pierre died. In 1906 she took over his teaching position at the Sorbonne, becoming the first woman to teach there.
  • She showed up when it mattered. During WWI she didn't wait around, she personally got X-ray equipment out to soldiers who needed it.

The Only Woman in the Room

In 1927, Marie Curie was invited to the Solvay Conference in Brussels, a meeting of the top physicists of the time. In the famous group photo from that conference, she's the only woman, sitting just two seats away from Einstein.

It wasn't easy for her to even get that kind of recognition. In 1911, the French Academy of Sciences rejected her application for membership by just two votes, only months before she won her second Nobel Prize. She never let that stop her, and that's part of why she's still such a big deal for women in science today.
